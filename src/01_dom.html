FILE UNDER · THE ROLL OF THE ORDER — WHAT WAS CARRIED, WAITS

What Mara Voss tells every new Waylighter at the fire

read once · the rest you learn by walking
M. VOSS · HAVEN
Every station is short of something another one makes. The terminal board says who is out of what; a crate is worth more the worse they need it, and several crates one way pay more together. Accept, walk, press E at the station named. Click anywhere to walk there — click a terminal to walk in and use it; hold both mouse buttons to sprint. W A S D when you want your hands on the wheel. The gold line at the top of the screen always says what to do next and points the way.
Connection is a wire, not a light. Enough deliveries and a station joins the wire: from then on what it makes moves to the others without you — faster over roads you pave and routes you charter — and the quiet pulls back from its ground. Let a station on the wire run dry and it dims, loses people, and falls silent again. Deliveries earn ◆ trust — trust is what a station remembers your name by.
You are mortal. Scrappers, wraiths, rivers and the cold can end you. When you fall, everything you carried waits where you fell — and the next Waylighter inherits the whole network.
Your body learns. Walking, hauling, steadying, building, salvaging — every verb feeds a skill. Press K to read what your body knows. Your calling learns its skills twice as fast.
Eat. Food is the thin bar under your load. H eats. Terminals print rations from provisions; farms donate them.
Waylighters wait by station fires. Hire one to walk with you — they carry, steady you, and haul your cargo home if you die. Charter them onto routes you have made safe. Save one in the field and they never charge you.
The Grey keeps walkers who die inside it. Light frees them. Your journal transcribes at every terminal — recover it and your skills come back with it.

somebody has to walk it

Eight settlements in one valley, and nobody has heard from anybody in a year.

The mist between them grows where nothing moves. People call it the quiet. It takes roads first, then names, then whoever stays put long enough.

A farm with no medicine. A mill with no food. A hundred people at the Pharos nobody has reached. Every one of them has something another one is dying without.

There used to be an answer: the Order of Waylighters — coats, lanterns, an oath, a dispatch bell on every terminal. Loud, corny, and it worked. The quiet ate them one road at a time, and the last coat has hung by Haven’s door for three years.

Today Mara Voss takes it off the hook, looks you over once, and holds it out. “It’ll fit,” she says. “It always fits somebody.”

Somebody has to walk it.
